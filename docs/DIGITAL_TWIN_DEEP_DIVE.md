# What a factory digital twin is still missing — and a human-centred tracking, error, delivery and control component

*Scope: a research review of the concept of a factory digital twin, measured against the public standards and the human-reliability literature and against what this repository actually establishes at v3.51, followed by the design contract for the four releases that close part of the gap (v3.53–v3.56). Written 2026-09-23. Everything below is informed by the cited standards and papers, not a certification, not a validated model of any real plant, and not a claim that the capabilities of chapters 6–9 already exist: chapter 3 says what exists, chapters 6–9 say what is planned and how it will be proved.*

> **Honesty preamble.** Every number in this document is either cited to a public source with its edition, or labelled a *teaching value*. The simulation it discusses is synthetic and seeded; standards are used as vocabulary and as design constraints, never as a conformance claim; adverse findings are kept. "Human" has a precise meaning here: an error belongs to a process step and to a latent condition of the workplace, never to a named person; the model aggregates by step and station, never by worker; the German works-constitution and data-protection rules are treated as design constraints of the software, not as compliance footnotes.

## 1. Why this document

The question was put plainly: *what is missing in the concept of designing a digital twin of a factory, and what would a component look like that tracks every package, keeps a database of everything, takes human error and the delivery and shipping times in between into account, and stays human?*

The first honest answer is that this repository does not contain a digital twin, and neither does most software that carries the name. It contains a **digital model** — a deterministic, seeded, synthetic simulation of a warehouse or a factory line — with, since v3.50, one **digital shadow**: a machining cell whose two cycle times are measured on a public dataset and flow one way, from the data into the model. Nothing flows back. That is the vocabulary of Kritzinger et al. (2018), and it is worth holding on to, because the word "twin" is used so loosely that it stops meaning anything.

The second honest answer is that the concept itself, as it is usually presented, is missing four things that matter more than fidelity of rendering: an **event history** (what happened to which unit, when, where and why — not the state of the plant at a moment, but its record), a **quality dimension** (errors, rework, damage, the loop from inspection back to the step that produced the fault), the **outside** (goods do not appear at the dock and vanish at the other dock; they arrive late in trailers and leave in carriers with promises attached), and **people** (not as walking figures, but as the part of the system whose work is shaped by lighting, labels, time pressure and the design of the step — and whose data must not be collected in ways the law and decency forbid).

This document reviews the concept against the standards (chapter 2), inventories what the app has (chapter 3), lists twelve gaps with the standard each violates and the cost of not closing it (chapter 4), and then spends three chapters on the human question before it designs anything (chapter 5). Chapters 6 to 9 are design contracts: the tracking database, the error model, the delivery model and the control tower, each with the harness checks that will prove it. Chapter 10 is about credibility, chapter 11 about what comes after, chapter 12 is the register.

Four rules govern the whole thing, and they are the repository's own (README, "Honesty & standards"): all data is synthetic and seeded unless the user imports their own; standards inform, they do not certify; the numbers are modelled, not measured, except where a measurement is named with its rule and source; and there are no superlatives. A fifth rule is added here for the human chapters and binds every line of code that follows: **errors belong to steps, not to people; aggregates only; nothing keyed to a worker.**

## 2. What a factory digital twin is, by the standards

### 2.1 ISO 23247: the definition and the observable manufacturing elements

ISO 23247 (parts 1–4, 2021) is the manufacturing digital-twin framework standard. Its definition is short: a digital twin in manufacturing is *a fit-for-purpose digital representation of an observable manufacturing element with synchronization between the element and its digital representation*. Three words carry the weight. **Fit-for-purpose** — a twin is not a copy; it represents what the purpose needs and omits the rest, which is why a rendering of every bolt is neither necessary nor sufficient. **Observable** — the thing represented must be observable in the physical world: an element that no sensor, scan or record touches cannot have a twin, only a model. **Synchronization** — there is a contract about how the representation is kept in step with the element: how often, how stale it may be, in which direction.

Part 2 gives a reference architecture in four domains: the *observable manufacturing domain* (the plant), the *device communication domain* (how observations leave the plant), the *digital twin domain* (the representation and its services) and the *user domain* (the people and applications that use it). Part 1 lists the kinds of **observable manufacturing element (OME)**: personnel, equipment, materials, processes, facilities, environment, products, and supporting documents. Two further parts are in preparation: part 5 on the digital thread and part 6 on composition of twins — evidence that the standard itself regards the record over time (the thread) and the assembly of twins from twins as unfinished business.

The list of OMEs is the most useful thing in the standard for a review like this one, because it turns "is it a twin?" into eight smaller questions: for each kind of element, is it represented, is it observable, is it synchronised, and for what purpose? Chapter 3 answers them for this app; the answers are uncomfortable in the right way.

### 2.2 Kritzinger's ladder: model, shadow, twin

Kritzinger, Karner, Traar, Henjes and Sihn (IFAC INCOM 2018) reviewed the manufacturing literature and found that authors used "digital twin" for three different things, distinguished by the level of automation of the data flow between the physical and the digital object:

- a **digital model** — a representation with no automated data exchange in either direction; changes on one side are carried to the other by hand, if at all;
- a **digital shadow** — an automated one-way flow from the physical to the digital object; the representation follows the plant, the plant does not follow the representation;
- a **digital twin** — automated flow in both directions; a change in the digital object can change the physical one.

Their finding was that real twins in that strict sense were scarce in the literature; most of what was published were models and shadows. The ladder is the second measuring stick used here. By it, this app was a digital model until v3.50, and it is now a model with one shadow: the NIST box-assembly cell's two machining cycles are the medians of measured program run times (the reduction rule is stated in `docs/NIST_BOX_ASSEMBLY.md`), retrieved once by a tool, not streamed. Nothing the app computes is ever sent anywhere. The releases planned in this document did not change that classification. **v3.58 moved it by one manual step:** the tracking store and the SQL tool accept a recorded EPCIS 2.0 capture document (chapter 11, item 1; [EPCIS_IMPORT.md](EPCIS_IMPORT.md)), so recorded events from a plant can be asked the same questions as the twins - but a file is carried by hand, not streamed, so the app is a model with a *manual* shadow, still not an automated one. Chapter 11 says what would make it automated.

### 2.3 The Asset Administration Shell: identity and interoperability

IEC 63278-1:2023 standardises the **Asset Administration Shell** (AAS), the structure of a digital representation of an industrial asset that came out of Germany's Plattform Industrie 4.0. The AAS answers a question ISO 23247 leaves open: how two pieces of software agree on *which* asset they are talking about and *what* its properties, documents and capabilities are, in a form that can be exchanged. An AAS has an identifier, submodels (nameplate, technical data, documentation, operational data) and a defined way to serialise and exchange them.

This matters for a review because a twin without stable, exchangeable identities is a twin that cannot be joined to anything outside itself. Chapter 3 shows that the app's identities are internal: an element id inside a layout, a handling-unit id inside a run, a GS1-shaped SSCC that is *serialised from the unit's sequence number* and therefore recurs from run to run. They are consistent, deterministic and useful for reproduction — and they are not asset identities in the AAS sense. This is gap 7 in chapter 4 and it is documented rather than closed.

### 2.4 The vocabulary of what happened: GS1 EPCIS 2.0 and the Core Business Vocabulary

A digital thread is a record of events. GS1's **EPCIS 2.0** (ratified 2022) is the standard for exactly that record in supply chains: an event says *what* (the identifiers of the objects — SSCC for a logistic unit, SGTIN for a trade item instance, a quantity of an SGTIN class), *when* (an event time), *where* (a read point and a business location, usually GLNs), and *why* (a **business step**, a **disposition** after the event, and the business transactions the objects belong to). Five event types exist: the *ObjectEvent* (something happened to objects), the *AggregationEvent* (objects were put into or taken out of a container — cases onto a pallet), the *TransformationEvent* (inputs were consumed and outputs produced — a manufacturing step), the *TransactionEvent* and, new in 2.0, the *AssociationEvent* (a sensor attached to a pallet).

The **Core Business Vocabulary 2.0** (June 2022) fixes the values: business steps such as `receiving`, `storing`, `stocking`, `picking`, `packing`, `staging_outbound`, `loading`, `shipping`, `inspecting`, `repackaging`, `unpacking`, `destroying`, `holding`, `arriving`, `departing`, `transporting`, and dispositions such as `in_progress`, `in_transit`, `returned`, `damaged`, `non_sellable_other`, `sellable_accessible`, `sellable_not_accessible`, `mismatch_class`, `mismatch_quantity`, `non_conformant`, `expired`, `recalled`, `unknown`. Every one of those identifiers was checked against the ratified CBV 2.0 text before it was used in chapter 6.

Two things follow. First, a factory or warehouse tracking database does not need an invented vocabulary; the vocabulary exists, it is public, and using it is what makes a record exchangeable. Second, a **simulation cannot produce a conformant EPCIS document**: EPCIS requires a wall-clock event time, and a synthetic run has only ticks. The tracking database in chapter 6 is therefore *EPCIS-shaped* — it carries the standard's fields and vocabulary and says in its header what it is not. That is the difference between using a standard and claiming it.

### 2.5 The names of the numbers: ISO 22400 and OTIF

ISO 22400-2:2014 defines 34 key performance indicators for manufacturing operations management at the ISA-95 level 3, each with a formula, elements, unit and time behaviour. The app already cites it for throughput and utilisation. What it does not yet use is the standard's **quality** group: *scrap ratio*, *rework ratio*, *first pass yield*, *quality ratio* — the KPIs that exist only if the model has a notion of a unit going wrong. Chapter 7 uses the standard's names for the new quality KPIs and the standard's definitions for what counts as reworked and scrapped.

Service to the customer is not in ISO 22400; the working definition of **on-time in-full (OTIF, also DIFOT)** — the share of orders delivered both on time and complete — is used in chapter 8 as commonly stated in logistics practice, with the explicit note that a 95 % target quoted by carriers is a convention, not a standard.

### 2.6 The German planning frame: VDI 3633, VDI 4499, VDI 5200

Three VDI guidelines place all of this in the tradition the app's users work in. **VDI 3633 Blatt 1** (simulation of logistics, material-flow and production systems — fundamentals) describes what a simulation study is, and in particular that a model must be **verified** (built right) and **validated** (the right model) before its results are used, with documented assumptions. **VDI 4499 Blatt 1** (digital factory — fundamentals, 2008) predates the twin vocabulary and describes the digital factory as the network of models, methods and tools that plan and operate a factory together, integrated by data management. **VDI 5200 Blatt 1** (factory planning — planning procedure) gives the seven phases from target definition to ramp-up support. The app's honest place in that frame: a teaching-scale tool for the concept and detailed-planning phases that verifies itself by harness and self-test (chapter 10), and validates nothing against a real plant because it has no real plant.

### 2.7 Where the app stands

Measured by ISO 23247: a fit-for-purpose representation of equipment, materials, processes and facilities, illustrative for personnel, absent for environment and documents, with no synchronisation contract because there is nothing to synchronise with. Measured by Kritzinger: a model with one shadow. Measured by the AAS: internal identities only. Measured by EPCIS: a state machine per unit with a consistent, private event record (the run ledger) but no standard vocabulary for what happened. Measured by ISO 22400: throughput and utilisation, no quality. That is the starting point of chapter 3.

## 3. What this app already has

The inventory below is the **state at v3.56**, the last release of the programme this document opened at v3.51: chapters 6 to 9 were written as design contracts and have shipped (v3.53 the tracking database, v3.54 human error, v3.55 delivery windows, v3.56 the control tower); this chapter was rewritten when they did. File names are given so a reader can check them; every claim has a harness named in the Reproduce section.

### 3.1 The run ledger: identities, quantities, timing, staffing, costs - and now errors, windows and decisions

Every live material-flow run is recorded by a **pure observer** (`ledger.js`, schema `factory-run-ledger/v1`) on the simulation's single after-tick hook; a run is byte-identical with or without it, and the four committed exports of v3.46 are byte-identical at v3.56 (asserted by every harness of the programme). The record holds one handling unit per order line with GS1-shaped identities (SSCC, GTIN-13, GTIN-14 on GS1's documentation prefix), the conserved quantities of the packaging profile at every operation, the tick of every event, the stations' service times, the rates a unit was costed at, the staffing changes of the adaptive policy - and, since this programme: the **error** a unit's branch realised (`error_kind`, `error_op`, `error_outcome`, the latent levers), its **delivery** facts (the trailer it came off, the due tick, the transit, the customer tick, on time shipped, on time in full) and the **control tower's audit** (the decisions a person took, with the run each came from). `stats()` answers per order type and, when the what-ifs ran, per operation (first pass yield, rework ratio, scrap ratio - ISO 22400-2's names) and per run (OTIF, shipped on time, the trailers).

The **tracking twins** (`tracking.js`, v3.53) are a second view of the same events, never a second recorder: every handling event has exactly one EPCIS-shaped twin - GS1 EPCIS 2.0 event types and actions, CBV 2.0 business steps and dispositions used as vocabulary - derived by a pure function of the ledger (`fromLedger`, or incrementally `observe`) and again by a Python twin of the same mapping when the SQLite tool imports a file, the two pinned equal on the committed fixture. Depalletise, pack and palletise are aggregation events over the ledger's own quantities; a return enters `returned`; an error shows as `mismatch_class`, `sellable_not_accessible` or `damaged` at the step and `inspecting` with `wt:error.detected` at the verification; the shipping event carries `wt:delivery`. There is no wall clock, and the export says so. A store in the browser (IndexedDB over http, an in-memory fallback elsewhere) keeps the last twenty runs and answers *where was unit X* across runs by handling-unit id or order reference - not by SSCC, which recurs per run.

The **SQLite twin** (`tools/run_ledger.py`) imports an export into fifteen tables and exposes **37 views**: the planner's questions (cycle time, touches, waits, quantities, dispatch, cost, flow links, staffing, dwell per business step, quality by step, on time in full, the trailers, the control tower's audit), the detail views, the cost views, the compare views, the replication views (which key their groups on the error and delivery levers as well as the staffing policy, so runs with different what-ifs never pose as replications) and five invariants that must return no rows (conservation, cross-dock, versions, terminals, tracking gaps). `reconcile` measures SQLite against the JavaScript rows of twenty views column by column; the viewer (`run-ledger.html`) draws every table by the SQL it shows.

### 3.2 The flow model, the routing model, the line model

The **flow simulation** (`flowsim.js`) is a deterministic, seeded, tick-based model - one tick a minute - of handling units moving along routes between anchors, queueing at stations whose service rates are the declared capacities of the WMS stage model, with a quota dispatcher that sends units down declared branches exactly and replayably. Three what-ifs join the staffing policy of v3.45, each a run input that changes the run id and each absent by default so that everything before is byte-identical: **human error** (v3.54: declared shares per step multiplied by three HEART-anchored levers, realised by `routing.branchesFor` as one extra branch per kind and step - an unrolled rework through two new detection operations, or a write-off), **delivery windows** (v3.55: trailers scheduled on a period and late by a Weyl sequence through the lateness quantiles of a public delivery dataset, the door open a fixed time, units spawning only while it is open; carriers departing on a period, a loaded unit waiting for the next) and the **control tower** (v3.56: a fourth observer on the same hook that proposes a lever from aggregates and waits). The routing model's own KNOWN LIMITS still hold and gained one sentence: a rework is a detour, not a loop; a unit errs at most once; cycles need a directed graph.

The **factory line model** (`process.js`) is a Theory-of-Constraints line: operations with cycle times and servers, precedence, optional multi-way routing with declared split ratios, takt, bottleneck, line efficiency, Little's-law WIP, and a provenance per operation - the NIST box-assembly cell's cycles are measured (medians of program run times, the reduction rule and the unusable logs reported); everything else is a labelled teaching value.

### 3.3 The people and the goods

The workforce (`workers.js`, `shift.js`) is an **illustrative roster**: one drawn worker per manned element, a pose per task, a gait driven by travel, a read-only congestion store that smooths the queue signal for the drawing. Nothing in the ledger, the tracking twins, the error model or the control tower is keyed to a worker, and four harnesses assert that the modules reference no roster: an error belongs to a step and a latent condition, a proposal's evidence is element ids and counts (chapter 5). The goods (`goods.js`, `pack.js`) are drawn in the form the operation gives them - pallet load, carton, tote, parcel - with one cosmetic limit stated in v3.54: a unit queued for its re-pick is drawn in its first-pass form.

### 3.4 What is measured and what is not

Three things in the app are measured on public data: the two machining cycles of the NIST box-assembly cell (v3.50), the board-grade table's unit arithmetic (v3.47), and - since v3.55 - the **shape** of delivery lateness by shipment mode from the USAID SCMS delivery history (10 324 shipment lines reduced to per-mode quantiles by a stated nearest-rank rule; the licence recorded as unresolved with the reason; aggregates only). Modelled with a cited anchor: the error shares and the levers (HEART generic task types and error-producing conditions, SPAR-H nominal values - nuclear-industry anchors used as teaching values). Modelled without an anchor and labelled: the assembly and inspection cycles, the periods, the promised lead, the nominal transit, the OTIF target, the control tower's thresholds. Unknown and said to be: every plant this model has not seen.

**Design inference.** The gaps sort themselves. Four are about the *record* (1, 2, 7, 9), three about *quality* (3, 5, 8), two about the *outside* (4, 8), one about the *return path* (6) and two about *people* (11, 12). The releases follow that order because each one needs the vocabulary of the one before it: errors are dispositions, dispositions need events, lateness is an event's timing against a promise, and a control tower reads all of it.

## 5. Keeping it human

### 5.1 Why the picker is not a server

Every station in the current model is a server: it has a rate, a queue and nothing else. Grosse, Glock, Jaber and Neumann (IJPR 2015) reviewed the order-picking planning literature and found that almost none of it represented the person doing the picking, and proposed a framework of four kinds of human factor that planning models omit: **perceptual** (can the picker see and read the label, the light, the display), **mental** (attention, memory load, the number of things to keep in mind per line), **physical** (posture, reach, weight, walking), and **psychosocial** (autonomy, time pressure, monotony). Their 2017 content analysis found learning curves and fatigue effects documented in the literature but rarely in the models, and error treated, when at all, as a constant rate. The consequence for a digital twin is direct: a station that is a server predicts the throughput of a plant with no people in it. The corrections are not exotic — a learning curve, a fatigue curve, an error probability that depends on the design of the step — and the third of them is what this plan builds first, because it is the one that changes what the plant *delivers*, not only how fast. *The other two followed in v3.66 ([PEOPLE_CURVES.md](PEOPLE_CURVES.md)): a station is no longer only a rate and a queue. It gets faster as its own cumulative units accumulate (Wright's rate in Crawford's unit form, floored where a bench stops improving) and slower as the minutes since its last break add up (an uplift shaped by work-study rest allowances, the break assumed staggered). Both are declared, both belong to the step and the shift, and neither may be attached to a person — which is the whole argument of this chapter, made mechanical.*

### 5.2 A taxonomy of error that does not blame

Rasmussen (1983) distinguished three levels of behaviour: **skill-based** (automatic, highly practised sensorimotor routines — reaching for the tote), **rule-based** (stored if-then rules — "if the label says 12, pick 12"), and **knowledge-based** (novel situations reasoned through). Errors differ by level: *slips* and *lapses* at the skill level (the right intention, the wrong execution or a forgotten step), *mistakes* at the rule and knowledge levels (a wrong intention). A mis-pick of the adjacent SKU is usually a slip; a wrong put-away into the slot that "looked right" is a rule-based mistake; a mis-scan is a slip. The distinction matters because the remedies differ — a slip is prevented by the design of the step (a check, a distinct label, a light), a rule-based mistake by the rule and the training.

Reason (1990) added the second half: an **active failure** at the sharp end happens because **latent conditions** at the blunt end allowed it — the two similar SKUs slotted next to each other, the label printed at low contrast, the shift that ran long, the target that rewarded speed over the check. His Swiss-cheese picture, layers of defence each with holes, says that an error that reaches the customer passed through every layer; the question is never "who" but "which holes lined up". That is the taxonomy the error model uses: every error is a `{kind, step, latent[]}` triple, the latent conditions being the levers whose multipliers were above one when it happened.

### 5.3 The performance-shaping factors as design levers

SPAR-H (NUREG/CR-6883) modifies its nominal human error probabilities by eight performance-shaping factors: available time, stress and stressors, complexity, experience and training, procedures, ergonomics and the human-machine interface, fitness for duty, and work processes. HEART (Williams 1986) does the same with 38 error-producing conditions, three of which are quoted in chapter 7 with their maximum multipliers: unfamiliarity with the situation (×17), shortage of time for error detection and correction (×11), and a low signal-to-noise ratio (×10). Read as engineering rather than as blame, every one of those is a **design lever of the step**: the label's contrast and the lighting are the signal-to-noise ratio; the adjacency of look-alike SKUs and the training plan are familiarity; the target rate and the length of the shift are time pressure; the scan-verification is the procedure that gives an error a chance to be caught before it leaves the step. Three of the eight become editable multipliers in the knowledge base (R20); the other five are documented and not modelled, with the reason: fitness for duty and stress cannot be represented without representing a person, and that is precisely what this design refuses to do.

The anchors themselves need their caveat repeated wherever they are used. SPAR-H and HEART were built for nuclear and process-industry safety analysis, on tasks and consequences unlike a warehouse pick. Their nominal values are cited here because they are the only *generic*, public, documented human-error probabilities that exist; the trade figures quoted by warehouse-technology vendors ("99.6 % accuracy with pick-to-light") are marketing claims without a method. So every share in the model is a **teaching value** anchored on a HEART generic task type, labelled as such in the code, in the knowledge base and in every export, and editable by the person who knows their plant.

### 5.4 What may be measured, and what must not

The German Works Constitution Act gives the works council a right of co-determination over *the introduction and use of technical devices designed to monitor the behaviour or performance of employees* (BetrVG § 87 Abs. 1 Nr. 6). The courts read "designed to" as *capable of*: the mere possibility that a device records something from which a person's performance could be derived triggers the right, regardless of whether anyone intends to use it that way. A handheld scanner, a pick-to-light system and a WMS that timestamps every pick are all such devices, and a tracking database that stores which worker scanned what, when, is one too. GDPR Art. 88 adds that employee data processing needs a specific legal basis and safeguards for dignity and legitimate interests. Industry 5.0 (European Commission, 2021) says the same from the other direction: human-centric means the technology serves the worker's well-being, not the other way round.

The design consequence is a rule, not an aspiration, and it is enforced by harness: **the tracking database keys every event to a handling unit, an element and a business step, never to a worker; the illustrative roster is never joined to events; error counts are aggregated per step and per shift; no rate, wait or error is ever attributed to a person.** A plant that adopted the model would still have to take the real device — the scanner — to its works council; what the model can promise is that it gives them nothing to argue about.

### 5.5 What "human in the loop" means for the control tower

A control tower that acts on its own is an automation of judgement; one that proposes and explains is a support of it. Chapter 9's tower does the second: it reads aggregates (a station's queue held above a threshold for a sustained period; a step's rework share against its declared share; a trailer's lateness; the on-time share), states the evidence, the lever it proposes, the effect it expects and why, and waits. Accepting re-runs the day with the lever applied — the run id changes, because a run's inputs changed — and the decision is written to the audit trail. Declining costs nothing and is never overridden. Every proposal is explainable from numbers a works council can see, because no other numbers exist.

## 6. The tracking database (design contract for v3.53)

### 6.1 Derived, not recorded

The tracking database is a **second view of the run ledger**, not a second recorder. Every handling event the ledger already writes has exactly one tracking twin, produced by a pure function of the ledger export (`WT.tracking.fromLedger(exp)`) or incrementally as the run proceeds (`observe(track, rec)` maps the events the ledger appended since the last tick). This has three consequences that are worth their cost: the four committed run exports do not change by a byte; the SQLite tool can derive the tracking rows at import from the handling events it already has, with a Python twin of the mapping that a test pins equal to the JavaScript one; and the mapping can be argued about, corrected and re-derived without ever touching a run.

### 6.2 The event

An event is EPCIS-shaped: `what` is the unit's SSCC (or, for an aggregation, a parent SSCC and a child quantity list of GTIN-14 cases or GTIN-13 eaches), `when` is the tick and the minute (and, honestly, `eventTime: null` — a simulation has no wall clock, which is why the export is EPCIS-*shaped* and says so in its header), `where` is a read point (a GLN derived deterministically from the element id through the shipped `ids.gln`, or a zone with no GLN when the ledger only knows the zone) and a business location (the stage zone), and `why` is a CBV business step, a CBV disposition after the event, and the business transaction (the order, typed `po`, or `rma` for a return, plus the user's order reference when the run was fed from an order file). Two extension fields, prefixed `wt:`, carry what CBV has no word for: the ledger's own kind (so that a *queued* event — a unit waiting, which CBV does not name — is kept and dwell can separate waiting from processing) and, from v3.54 and v3.55, the error and the delivery facts.

### 6.4 The aggregation tree, persistence and eviction

Pallet ⊃ cases ⊃ eaches, parcel ⊃ eaches: the tree is what `pack.js` already computes for every route (its conservation identity — eaches carried plus retained plus scrapped equals received — holds at every step), so the aggregation events only *name* it. Persistence across runs is a versioned IndexedDB database (`wt-tracking-v1`: a `runs` store keyed by run id and an `events` store keyed by event id with indexes by run, by unit and by order reference), with a stored-sequence counter instead of a clock, eviction of the oldest run beyond a cap of twenty, export as `factory-tracking-events/v1`, and an import that validates the schema and refuses an unknown business step. Where IndexedDB is not available — Node, a file:// load, a private window — the same promise-shaped API is served by an in-memory store, so the mapping and the queries are proven in Node and the persistence is proven in the headless browser self-test. A note on identity that the standards make unavoidable: the SSCC recurs from run to run because it is serialised from the unit's sequence number, so "history across runs" is keyed by the handling-unit id (which embeds the run id) or by the order reference — the same order file replayed under different seeds and levers — and not by the SSCC (gap 7).

### 6.5 The SQLite twin and the three questions

Four views: `v_epcis_events` (the twins joined to their units), `v_unit_history` (one unit's steps with the ticks to the next), `v_bizstep_dwell` (per business step: events, units, mean and maximum dwell, the waiting share — reconciled between SQL and JavaScript), and the invariant `v_tracking_gaps` (a handling event without a twin, or a twin whose step is outside the CBV list — must return no rows). The three questions the database answers are the ones a planner asks first: *where was unit X and what happened to it* (`historyOf`), *at which step do units wait and for how long* (`dwellByBizStep`), and, once v3.54 ships, *which steps produce the dispositions that are not `in_progress`* (`dispositionCounts`).

**Acceptance gate:** `verify_tracking.js` — one twin per handling event; the first event of every unit an ADD at `receiving` or `picking`; the terminals mapped as the table; an unpacking event whose child list is exactly the cases per pallet; a returned unit entering as `returned` with an `rma` transaction; every emitted identifier in the CBV list and named in chapter 12; valid GLNs one-to-one with elements; the dwell equal to the ledger's own spans; store round-trip, eviction and the refusal of an unknown step; a byte-identical export over two identical runs with no wall clock; an incremental observer that never mutates the ledger or the sim; no worker reference, no clock; the shipped wiring.

## 7. Human error in the model (design contract for v3.54)

### 7.1 An error is a change of disposition at a step

In the tracking vocabulary an error is exactly what it is in a real plant: at a step, a unit's disposition changes from `in_progress` to something that will not do — `mismatch_class` for a mis-pick, `sellable_not_accessible` for a put-away into the wrong slot, `damaged` for a handling damage — with a probability that belongs to the step and to the conditions under which it is performed. The model writes that as **p(step) = share(kind) × Π multiplier(lever)**, capped at one half so that the base branch always keeps a positive share. The shares are teaching values anchored on HEART generic task types: a mis-pick at 0.02 (HEART's "routine, highly practised, rapid task involving relatively low skill"), a wrong put-away at 0.003 ("restore or shift a system to original or new state following procedures, with some checking"), a handling damage at 0.005 (a teaching value with no generic anchor — no human-error probability covers damage, and the code says so). The levers are three HEART error-producing conditions with their maximum multipliers as the top of the editable range: time pressure (×11), signal-to-noise — label contrast and lighting (×10), familiarity — training and look-alike adjacency (×17). All seven numbers live in a new knowledge-base category, *human factors (teaching values)*, whose description repeats the rule of chapter 5.

### 7.2 Detection and the unrolled rework

An error that nobody sees is a customer complaint; an error that a check catches is rework. The model gives every error kind its **check**: a mis-pick is detected at a *pick verification* step (the scan check at the face) and the pick is done again; a wrong put-away is detected at a *put-away verification* (the location scan) and the put-away is done again; a damage is detected at the step and the unit goes to scrap through the returns bench. These are the design levers of poka-yoke — Shingo's mistake-proofing — and they cost what they cost: one more service at the bench and the wait in front of it, which the ledger's cost model charges exactly as it charges any other service.

The rework is **unrolled**, not looped: the error route re-lists the operation once after its verification (`… pick, verify-pick, pick, …`) and continues; a unit that errs twice is not modelled, and the routing model's known limit — cycles need a directed graph — stays exactly where it was, with one sentence added. The alternative, promoting the operation list to a graph, is the honest long-term fix and would invalidate the hand-computed per-route expectations of the routing harness; it is deferred and named.

### 7.3 Realisation: branches dispatched by quota, never a random draw

The existing returns split is the precedent: a declared share becomes a **branch** with its own route, and units are sent down the branches by a largest-remaining-quota dispatcher that is exact to within one unit and replayable. Errors are realised the same way, at plan time and only when the error what-if is switched on: for every base branch of an archetype and every error kind that applies to one of its operations, a branch `{kind, op, share = base × effective, ops with the rework spliced in}` is added, and the base branch keeps `1 − Σ effective`. Without the what-if the branch list is what it was — byte for byte — which is what the routing harness's collapse checks verify. The dispatcher's arithmetic is hand-checkable and is pinned: a 2 % share over 100 units errs at units 25 and 75 (0-based) and nowhere else, by the deficit rule *the k-th unit takes the branch whose quota is furthest behind, ties to the base*.

### 7.4 What the numbers are called

ISO 22400-2's quality KPIs, per operation and per run: **first pass yield** = units through the step without an error ÷ units through; **rework ratio** = reworked ÷ through; **scrap ratio** = scrapped for damage ÷ through. They live in the ledger's statistics, in a SQL view (`v_quality_by_step`), in the viewer's planner section and in the flow readout — and only when the what-if ran; a run without it shows "every step perfect", which is the honest description of the current model. The run id carries the error declaration in its hash, so a run with errors can never be mistaken for one without.

**Acceptance gate:** `verify_errors.js` — byte-identical plan without the what-if; the quota indices [25, 75] by hand; the piece-pick error route's exact operation list; the route count with the full default mix (nine base plus eleven error branches); unfulfillable damage branches on a floor without a returns bench, named; the PSF arithmetic and the cap; conservation over 600 ticks globally and per route; the reworked unit's collapsed events equal to its route; the cost of a mis-pick equal to one extra service plus the wait; the quality KPIs on the 100-unit run (2 errors, FPY 0.98); the error keys only on error routes; the tracking dispositions; the KB seeds equal to the routing defaults; the known-limits text kept and extended; no worker, no clock; the shipped wiring.

## 8. Delivery and shipping times in between (design contract for v3.55)

### 8.1 What the dataset gives, and what it does not

The USAID *Supply Chain Shipment Pricing Data* is the delivery history of the SCMS programme: 10 324 shipment lines of HIV/AIDS commodities to some forty countries between 2006 and 2015, with a shipment mode (air, truck, ocean, air charter), the date the purchase order was sent, the scheduled delivery date, the date delivered to the client and the date the delivery was recorded, plus weight and freight cost. It is a public dataset of *international pharmaceutical lanes*, and nothing about it transfers to a German plant's inbound trucks in scale — a lateness of nine days on an ocean lane says nothing about a truck that was due at 08:00. What does transfer is the **shape**: lateness is skewed, most deliveries are close to schedule, a tail is very late, a few are early. The rule is therefore *shape, not scale*: the tool reduces the dataset to lateness quantiles per shipment mode (min, p10, median, p90, max, and the shares late, early and on time), the model draws a deterministic sequence of arrivals through that distribution, and a declared **teaching parameter** — ticks per dataset day, default sixty, "one dataset day is one plant hour" — maps it onto the plant's clock. The parameter is in the knowledge base with that sentence next to it.

Two facts about the dataset are reported rather than hidden: some rows carry "Date Not Captured" or "N/A – From RDC" in a date field and are counted as unusable, never filled; and the dataset's licence could not be read from this machine (the USAID Development Data Library issues either a government-work licence, free of copyright, or a partner licence under CC BY-ND 4.0, per dataset). The tool records what the library's metadata says when it can reach it and "unresolved" otherwise — and in either case commits **only aggregates**, never a row, with the attribution the library asks for. Facts derived from a dataset with attribution are not the dataset.

### 8.2 Inbound: windows, trailers, a burst at the door

Inbound arrivals are modelled as **dock windows**: trailer *j* is scheduled at *j × P* ticks (P from the knowledge base, default 120), arrives *late_j* ticks later, and the door is open for O ticks (default 30) from its arrival; an early trailer waits for its slot. The lateness sequence is not random: `u_j` is the fractional part of *j × 0.618 033 988 7* (a Weyl sequence — evenly spread, deterministic, replayable), `late_j` is the piecewise-linear inverse of the mode's lateness distribution at `u_j`, scaled by the teaching parameter and rounded. Sixteen values are precomputed by the app from the dataset and the knowledge base and handed to the plan; the simulation itself has no dependency on the dataset and draws nothing from its random stream — the spawn accumulator simply waits while the door is closed and releases a burst when it opens, capped by the in-flight limit. This is the one place the model touches the tick loop, and it is a single guard between two existing guards, so that every existing run stays byte-identical and the finite-pool drain still completes (the windows are periodic and unbounded).

### 8.3 Outbound: departures, promises, on time or not

Outbound is the mirror: carriers depart every M ticks (default 240); a delivered unit waits at the dock for the next departure instead of the fixed eight-tick dwell (never less than it). Every unit is given a **due tick** at spawn — its spawn tick plus a promised lead (default 480 ticks, a teaching value) — and, when it leaves, a transit time drawn through the same shape rule, so that two on-time facts exist: *shipped on time* (it left the dock before its due tick) and *on time* (it would reach the customer before its due tick, with the estimated transit). **OTIF** is then the share of orders whose every unit was delivered — nothing scrapped, nothing still in flight — and on time; with synthetic one-line orders one unit is one order, and that is said. The knowledge base carries the 95 % target with the note that it is a convention.

### 8.4 What it is and what it is not

The windows and the departures are a *what-if* like the staffing policy: switched on by a picker, present in the run id only when declared, logged (every trailer with its scheduled, arrival and lateness ticks) and exported. The "in-between" inside the plant — the dwell at each step — the ledger already measures; this release adds the outside on both ends. It does not model a yard, a dock schedule with appointments, a carrier's own network, or the customer's calendar; the transit estimate is a shape borrowed from a dataset that measured something else, and it is labelled at every place it appears.

**Acceptance gate:** `verify_delivery.js` — byte-identical plan, state and 400-tick run without the keys; the Weyl sequence and the CDF inverse by hand; the window openings and the trailer log by hand over 500 ticks; the random stream untouched (no draw inside the spawn loop; identical `rngState` between identical windowed runs); a finite pool of forty drains with windows; every delivered unit's dwell at least eight and its retirement a departure tick; OTIF by hand on a six-unit pool; conservation unchanged; the dataset twin's quantiles monotone and shares summing to one; the ledger and SQL keys only with windows; no clock, no worker; the shipped wiring and the credits line.

## 9. The control tower — a human in the loop (design contract for v3.56)

### 9.1 Proposes, explains, waits

The control tower is a **rule engine in the shape of the app's compliance check**: pure functions over the run's record and the simulation's aggregate state, evaluated every ten ticks, each rule with a threshold the user can edit in the knowledge base, each firing at most once per run (or again after a snooze). It attaches to the same after-tick hook the ledger uses — the ledger first, then the tracking mapper, then the tower — and all three only read; a harness proves that a run with the tower attached is byte-identical to a run without. A rule's output is a **proposal**: the rule, the tick, the evidence (numbers and element ids — never anything else), the lever it proposes, the effect it expects and the reason. Four rules ship:

| Rule | Reads | Fires when | Proposes | Expected effect, honestly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Queue congestion** | stations whose queue has been at or above the congestion threshold at every evaluation for a sustained period (default 30 ticks), by element id | no staffing policy is active | the adaptive-staffing what-if (a second worker joins at the threshold, leaves after the cool-down) | "on the hand floor at the floor rate the second worker cut the longest queue from 19 to 17 with no fewer completions; your floor will differ — re-run to measure" |
| **Rework burden** | per step: reworked units, the extra service ticks charged, the rework share of units through | the share exceeds its ceiling (default 0.01) after at least 20 units | reset the largest performance-shaping lever above one — the latent condition, named | "the declared effective share falls from x to y; the rework spans leave the cost" (arithmetic from the knowledge base) |
| **Inbound late** | trailers whose lateness exceeds the limit (default 60 ticks), the late share so far | at least one late trailer | a second receiving schedule offset by half a period | "bursts halve; lateness is the dataset's and unchanged" |
| **OTIF below target** | delivered orders and their on-time-in-full share | at least 20 deliveries and the share below the target | carrier departures twice as frequent | "dock dwell before departure falls by at most half a period per unit; transit is unchanged" |

### 9.2 Accept, decline, snooze — and re-run the day

A person decides in a card in the Simulate drawer. **Decline** writes an audit row and nothing else. **Snooze** the same, and the rule may propose again after its snooze period. **Accept** is the only path that writes: it sets the corresponding lever exactly as if the person had used the picker (so the picker reflects it), appends the audit row with the run it came from, and **re-runs the day from tick zero** — because a run's identifier is a hash of its inputs, and a run that changed its inputs half way through would carry an identifier that lies. The audit trail travels with the export, into a SQL table and a view, and into the viewer's glance. Since v3.57 the person can also **revert** the last accepted lever from the audit: the value the lever had rides in the accepted row, the undo appends a row of status `reverted` naming the proposal, and the day re-runs once more. Nothing acts on its own; nothing is undone on the person's behalf; and because the tower reads only aggregates by step and station, every proposal is explainable with numbers a works council can see.

### 9.3 What it is not

It is not a manufacturing execution system, not a warehouse management system, not a scheduler, and not a certification of anything. Its four rules are teaching rules with editable thresholds; its expected effects are either measured on the hand floor (and say so) or arithmetic on declared values (and say so). It cannot see a person, and it never will in this design.

**Acceptance gate:** `verify_control.js` — the observer never mutates the sim or the ledger and a run with it attached is byte-identical; queue congestion on the hand floor proposes at tick 140, naming the put-away element (the queue reaches six at tick 97, dips across the tick-100 evaluation and is at or above six from 110 - the measured fact replaced the planned 97 + 30); rework burden with a time-pressure multiplier of 11 names the lever and the arithmetic 0.22 → 0.02; inbound late with the hand lateness list proposes after the late trailer's window opens; OTIF fires only after twenty deliveries and only below target, never without outbound windows; decisions produce audit rows, a snoozed proposal returns, a declined one does not, two identical runs give identical proposals; the list is a pure function of the history; no worker reference, evidence of element ids and counts only, the honesty text naming the two laws; the audit only in an export that has one; the SQL and viewer twins by hand; the shipped wiring, including that chapter 3 of this document was rewritten.

## 10. Validation and credibility

VDI 3633 Blatt 1 asks of a simulation study that its model be **verified** (does the implementation do what the model says?) and **validated** (does the model say what the system does?), with the assumptions written down. This repository can honestly claim the first and not the second. Verification is what the harnesses do: every documented behaviour has a headless test, most with hand-computed values (the quota indices, the window openings, tick 97), every release re-runs all of them and the two browser self-tests, and byte-identity with the previous version is asserted for everything a release did not intend to change. Validation would need a real plant; the repository has none and says so on every panel.

Between the two sits **credibility**, which is what a reader can actually assess: what is measured, what is modelled, what is unknown. Measured: the two machining cycles of the NIST cell (medians of program run times, with the reduction rule, the unusable logs and the min / median / max reported), and, from v3.55, the shape of delivery lateness by shipment mode (quantiles of a public dataset). Modelled with a cited anchor: the error shares and the multipliers (HEART / SPAR-H nominal values as teaching values). Modelled without an anchor, labelled: the assembly and inspection cycles, the dock and departure periods, the promised lead, the thresholds. Unknown and said to be: the spread of the NIST medians across instances (reported as min / max, not as intervals), the transfer of any of it to a real site. Replications over seeds (v3.46) give Student-t intervals over the model's own randomness and nothing else; they do not estimate model error.

The one uncertainty this document adds is honest about its own anchors: a HEART generic task type is a distribution, not a point (the 0.02 task type has bounds of roughly 0.007 to 0.045 in the source), and a warehouse pick is not the task Williams' data described. The error what-if is a way to ask "what if one pick in fifty went wrong under these conditions", not a prediction that it does.

## 11. Roadmap: from model to shadow to twin

What would move the app up Kritzinger's ladder, in the order it would have to happen:

1. **The return path for data (shadow).** An *import* of live events in the vocabulary the tracking database already speaks: an EPCIS 2.0 capture document from a WMS, an MTConnect stream reduced as the NIST tool reduces it, a delivery log in the SCMS columns. The mapping is the same; the source changes from "the simulation" to "the plant", and the header stops saying *shaped*. *Shipped in v3.58 as a manual import (`WT.tracking.fromEpcis`, `tools/epcis_import.py`): only ObjectEvent and AggregationEvent, only the steps this app knows, `source = imported` on every row, the header says* imported. *A stream would need the synchronisation contract of item 2; MTConnect and the SCMS columns are still open.*
2. **A synchronisation contract (gap 9).** How often the record is refreshed, how stale a value may be before the twin says so, which side wins on conflict; ISO 23247's word for the thing the app has never needed. *Shipped in v3.64 for the one direction that exists: every imported record carries the contract and the app measures its age against the budget, per business step, as of an instant the caller names. What is still missing is the other half of the word - a contract needs a counterpart that promises to refresh, and a file a person carries promises nothing.*
3. **Asset identities (gap 7).** Registered GS1 prefixes instead of 4012345; element ids that survive a layout edit; an Asset Administration Shell submodel per machine with its nameplate and its measured cycle times as operational data. This needs an owner of the prefixes and of the register, which is not a software task.
4. **Environment and documents.** Lighting and temperature as observable elements rather than as levers; work instructions and delivery notes as the documents ISO 23247 lists.
5. **The graph.** Promote the routing model's operation lists to a directed graph, so that loops (replenishment, rework, re-slotting) are loops; then hold-and-release for blocked stock, the four grades of a return, and the empties counter-flow, each of which the routing model already names as not modelled.
6. **People, still without persons.** Learning curves and fatigue per step and per shift, the remaining SPAR-H factors as levers, and — only with a works council's agreement and a legal basis — the day a real scanner's aggregate is allowed to replace a teaching value. *The first half shipped in v3.66: both curves are declared per step and per shift, the learning count lives on the station and resets every run, and the break is assumed staggered. What is still open is the other five factors, turnover, skill mix, and the fact that a crew does not start from zero every morning — a warm-up the model does not have.* *The mechanics of that day shipped in v3.59 (`tools/fit_rates.py`, [SITE_PROFILE.md](SITE_PROFILE.md)): a site profile fitted from recorded events - aggregates per step and per trailer, labelled* measured on …, n = … *- loads as an overlay of the knowledge base; whether a plant may record such aggregates is still the works council's and the legal basis's question, not the tool's.*

Nothing in that list requires abandoning the discipline of this repository: each step is a key that is absent by default, a harness that proves the collapse, and a label that says what is measured.

## What is deliberately not modelled

- **A second error on the same unit**, an error at a verification step, or an error that escapes to the customer: one error, detected at the next check, is the whole model. A high first pass yield obtained by hiding an undetected error would be a failure of this document, not a result.
- **Anything per person**: no worker id in any event, no per-worker rate, no fatigue or learning curve (both documented as future work, both only per step and per shift when they come).
- **A yard, dock appointments, carrier networks, customer calendars**: the windows are periodic and the lateness is a borrowed shape; the customer-side transit is an estimate labelled as such.
- **Dangerous goods, the cold chain as a routed zone, the empties counter-flow, second-stage sortation, dispatch-label application, hold-and-release, the four grades of a return, loops** — the routing model's own list, unchanged.
- **Equipment failure, MTBF / MTTR, maintenance**: the machine catalogue's cycle times are teaching values and the machines never break.
- **Environment and documents** as observable elements.
- **Conformant EPCIS**: the export has no wall-clock event time and says so.

This document establishes a review and a design contract.
